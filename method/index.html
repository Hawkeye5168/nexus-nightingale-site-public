Method and limits

How the evidence is assembled, how a claim is marked, and what a dossier does not prove. The method is public. The machinery is not the product.

Research use only. This page describes a research method. It is not clinical advice, a diagnosis, a treatment recommendation or a regulatory submission. No patient data is used. Every output requires independent experimental and clinical validation.

The question comes first

A dossier is not a longer report. It begins with a decision question: what choice does the reader need to make, and what evidence would change that choice? A document that cannot name the question is a summary, not a decision document.

That question determines the identity checks, the mechanism analysis, the structures examined, the trials included and the recommendation. It also determines what is left out. Scope is part of the method.

The evidence chain

01 · Identity

What is the object?

Resolve the disease, gene, protein, organism and accession against public reference data. Fail loudly when an identifier cannot be resolved. A wrong identity makes every later section wrong.

02 · Structure

What shape is known?

Record experimental structures, constructs, coverage and quality. Supervised recovery is labelled as reconstruction against a named reference; the separate research folding path is never presented as a production prediction.

03 · Mechanism and route

What is the biology doing?

Decide whether the problem is loss of function, gain of function, aggregation, haploinsufficiency, splicing, metabolism or unknown. Then route to a shortlist of thirteen possible intervention routes, not a default molecule.

04 · Evidence and review

What supports the claim?

Assemble primary literature, curated databases, trials, labels and natural-history evidence. Mark every claim. Put the strongest countercase in the document and ask an independent reviewer to attack it.

Source tiers

Not every source carries the same weight. The tier is visible so a reader can disagree precisely.

TierWhat it coversHow it may be used
T1Primary experimental or clinical evidence; official regulatory textCan support a factual or clinical claim, with its population and limitations stated
T2Peer-reviewed synthesis, guideline or curated databaseCan support context and consensus; the primary source is preferred for a decisive claim
T3Registry entry, preprint or conference abstractHypothesis generation and trial context; not a clinical claim on its own
T4Computational output, model score or expert opinionCan support a hypothesis or a research direction; cannot establish a biological fact

Claim status

Every substantive claim has an identifier, a statement, a status, a confidence, at least one source and a date. The status vocabulary is deliberately blunt.

StatusMeaning
CURRENTThe source supports the claim at the stated date and the context still matches.
STALEThe claim was supported, but a newer source, label, guideline or trial has moved the field.
CONFLICTCredible sources disagree; the disagreement is visible rather than averaged away.
UNVERIFIEDThe claim is not yet tied to a resolvable source. It is shown as a gap, not treated as true.
BLOCKEDThe source could not be reached or the record is access-controlled. The claim remains open.
RETRACTEDThe source or claim has been withdrawn. It is retained in the record so the correction is auditable.

New papers and trials enter a review queue. They do not silently rewrite a claim. A claim changes only when the source is checked and the dossier version is bumped.

The thirty-section dossier

The current benchmark is the Sabre-Tek Dossier Benchmark v1. Thirty sections is a floor for a full dossier, not a target to pad toward. If the evidence cannot support thirty substantive sections, the right answer is a shorter, honest document.

The sections are grouped into eight jobs: identity, mechanism, structure, evidence base, therapeutic landscape, trial and patient context, critical appraisal and decision. Every section carries a key finding, at least one paragraph of analysis and a falsifier — the evidence that would change its conclusion.

A full dossier carries at least six factual figures, six quantitative tables, thirty unique sources, twenty-five claims and eight red-team items. Figures and tables carry their own sources. If an exhibit already carries the citations, the section-level source line is suppressed. One citation block per exhibit. No source-only pages. No page without a job.

Release gates

A dossier does not leave because it looks finished. It leaves when the gates pass.

  • Source gate: every factual claim resolves to a named source, a date and a tier; every figure and table source resolves.
  • Evidence gate: the section count, figures, tables, claims, red-team items and depth blocks meet the benchmark.
  • Safety and scope gate: unsupported clinical claims, patient data and overreach are removed before release.
  • Compassion gate: the document is something we would be willing to show to the family it is about. If it is not, it is fixed, not shipped.

A failed gate is fixed. It is not bypassed, softened or hidden in a footnote.

Independent review

The dossier is a research product, and the people who know the disease should attack it. Reviewers receive the full dossier, the decision brief, the claim ledger and a one-page brief with ten questions. They are asked for criticism — not endorsement, funding or a collaboration commitment.

Review status may be published as “requested”, “in progress” or “completed”. Reviewer names and confidential comments are not published without consent. A review is not complete until every objection is either fixed or recorded as an open disagreement.

What this does not establish

Read this before using a dossier. A NEXUS dossier is a computational research product. It does not establish that any molecule treats any disease. It does not replace a clinician, a wet-lab experiment, a clinical trial, a regulatory review or independent scientific judgement.
  • Not clinical advice: no diagnosis, treatment, eligibility or dosing decision.
  • Not a regulatory submission: no approval, authorisation or endorsement is claimed.
  • Not efficacy evidence: a target, a mechanism or a designed molecule is not a clinical result.
  • Not patient data: no patient records, identifiers or individual outcomes are published.
  • Not a blind-prediction claim: supervised folding is a reconstruction against a named reference; the research path is reported separately.

How to read a dossier

  1. Read the decision page and the scope note first.
  2. Check the identity: disease, gene, protein, accession and inheritance.
  3. Check the mechanism: is it loss, gain, aggregation, haploinsufficiency or unknown?
  4. Check the structure: what is experimental, what is a construct, what is a model?
  5. Check the evidence: source tiers, dates, conflicts and gaps.
  6. Read the therapeutic landscape: what is approved, what is in trials, what has failed?
  7. Read the red team before the recommendation.
  8. Ask what one experiment or source would change the conclusion.

What we will not do

We will not invent a target, a source, a number or a mechanism. We will not hide a gap to make a page look complete. We will not present a research model as a medicine. We will not call a document clinically validated because it is well formatted. And we will not ask a reviewer for a favour and call it review.

The method is public. The evidence is visible. The limits are stated. The judgement stays with the scientist.
