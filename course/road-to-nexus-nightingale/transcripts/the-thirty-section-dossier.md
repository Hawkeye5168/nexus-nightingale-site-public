# The Thirty-Section Dossier

**Road to NEXUS Nightingale**  
Episode 03 · 10 November 2026  
Host: Sabre  
Style: long-form, conversational, evidence-first  
Scope: research update, not medical advice

---

**SABRE:**

A dossier is not a longer report.

That is the first thing I want to say, because it is the easiest thing to get wrong.

When people hear “thirty sections,” they imagine padding. They imagine a document that repeats itself until it looks expensive. They imagine a consultant’s binder: lots of paper, lots of headings, and a recommendation buried somewhere near the end.

That is not what we built.

The thirty-section dossier exists because a serious research question has at least thirty jobs to do. Identity. Genetics. Mechanism. Structure. Evidence. Trials. Safety. Gaps. A recommendation. A countercase. A next experiment. If you remove one of those jobs, the document gets thinner in a way that matters.

So today I want to walk through the new layout. Not the engine. Not the internals. The document itself: what is in it, why it is in that order, and what changed when we set the benchmark.

And I want to be clear from the start: this is a research document. It is not medical advice. It is not a diagnosis. It is not a treatment recommendation. It is not a regulatory submission. It is a cited dossier that a scientist can read, challenge, and use to design a better experiment.

That is the job.

Let me start with the problem the layout is trying to solve.

A scientist working on a rare disease or a difficult cancer does not usually suffer from a lack of information. They suffer from a lack of assembly. There are papers, registries, structures, trials, variants, reviews, conference abstracts, and a dozen databases that use different identifiers for the same thing. The information exists. It is just scattered across systems that were not designed to answer one question together.

So the first temptation is to build a search engine. The second temptation is to build a chatbot. Both can be useful. Neither is a dossier.

A search engine gives you a list. A chatbot gives you a paragraph. A dossier gives you a structured argument, with the evidence attached and the uncertainty visible.

That is the difference.

The new layout is built around four ideas.

First, every dossier has to answer a decision question, not just describe a disease.

Second, every factual statement has to resolve to a source, and every source has to be identifiable and dated.

Third, every section has to say what would change its conclusion. That is the falsifier. Without it, a section is just a summary.

Fourth, the document has to be navigable. A scientist should be able to find the identity page, the mechanism page, the trial landscape, the source register, and the decision page without reading the whole thing from the beginning.

Those four ideas shape the layout.

Let me describe the document from the front.

It starts with a cover. The cover is not decoration. It carries the disease name, the dossier ID, the version, the date, the classification, and the scope note. If a page is printed and separated from the rest, the cover still tells you what it is and what it is not.

Then comes the document-control block. That sounds bureaucratic, and it is meant to. It tells you which version you are holding, what supersedes what, when it was produced, and what standard it is claiming to meet. If two people are looking at two different versions, the document should make that obvious before they start arguing.

Then the contents page. It lists the sections, the figures and the tables. That matters more than it sounds. A long document without a map is not a reference document; it is a wall.

Then the method page. It explains how the evidence was assembled, how sources were tiered, how claims were status-marked, and what the document does not do. It is the page that stops a reader from mistaking a research product for a clinical opinion.

Then the sections.

The first group is identity. What is the disease? What are its synonyms? What is the gene? What is the inheritance pattern? What is the affected protein? What is the clinical burden? What is the diagnostic pathway? These are not warm-up questions. If you get the identity wrong, every later section is built on the wrong object.

The second group is mechanism. What is the causal biology? Is this a loss of function, a gain of function, a toxic aggregate, a haploinsufficiency, a splicing defect, a metabolic block? What is the evidence for that mechanism? What is still unknown? This is where a dossier earns its keep, because the mechanism determines whether a therapeutic route is protective or dangerous.

The third group is structure. What experimental structures exist? What do they cover? What is missing? What is the quality of the evidence? If a structure is a fragment, a construct, or a model, the dossier says so. It does not blur a crystal structure, a cryo-EM map, a predicted model and a cartoon into one confident picture.

The fourth group is the evidence base. What does the primary literature say? What do the curated databases say? What do the trials say? What do the natural-history studies say? What is the quality of the evidence? Where are the conflicts? Which claims are current, which are stale, which are unverified, which are blocked?

The fifth group is the therapeutic landscape. What routes are plausible? Small molecule, biologic, gene therapy, editing, RNA-directed approach, protein replacement, cell therapy, symptomatic care. What has been tried? What failed? What is in early development? What is approved, and for whom? What is the evidence for the route, as distinct from the evidence for the target?

The sixth group is the trial and patient context. What trials are recruiting? What phase? What endpoints? What biomarkers? What prior lines? What populations are included and excluded? This is research context. It is not an eligibility decision, and the dossier says that plainly.

The seventh group is the critical appraisal. What are the strongest objections? What would a sceptical reviewer say? What data would overturn the recommendation? What is missing? What is the next experiment?

And the eighth group is the decision. What is the recommendation? What are the alternatives? What are the conditions? What would change the recommendation? What should happen next, and who should do it?

That is the skeleton. Thirty sections is not an arbitrary number. It is the number of distinct jobs that kept appearing when we pressure-tested the document.

Now, the benchmark.

We did not want to say “this is a good dossier” and leave it at that. We wanted a standard that could fail. So we wrote one down.

The Sabre-Tek Dossier Benchmark v1 requires at least thirty substantive sections. At least six factual figures. At least six quantitative tables. At least thirty unique sources. At least twenty-five claims. At least eight red-team items. And every section must carry a key finding, at least one paragraph of analysis, and a falsifier.

That last requirement is the one that changed the document most.

It is easy to write a section called “Clinical burden” and fill it with a paragraph from a review. It is much harder to write: here is the key finding, here is what it means, and here is the evidence that would change it.

The falsifier forces the writer to take a position. It also gives the reviewer something to attack. That is the point. A dossier that cannot be attacked is not a dossier; it is a brochure.

The benchmark also requires figures and tables, because humans read visually as well as verbally. But the figures have to be factual diagrams, not decoration. A pathway diagram, a trial landscape, a structure coverage map, a diagnostic flow, a comparison table. Each one has to carry its sources and its own caption.

The tables have to be quantitative. Not a list of drug names with no numbers. A table should let a reader compare dose, endpoint, phase, population, result, and limitation.

The benchmark also requires a source register. Every source gets an ID, a title, a URL, a date and an evidence tier. The IDs are unique. The URLs are unique. If a source appears twice, one of them is removed. If a section only repeats the source list from the page before, the section is a defect.

That sounds obvious. It was not obvious until we rendered the document and read it on paper.

One of the first full drafts had a page that was essentially a source list repeated from the previous page. It looked fine in a word processor. It looked pointless in print. The fix was structural: if a section has a figure or a table that carries the citations, the section-level source line is suppressed. One citation block per exhibit. No source-only pages.

That is now a rule.

The benchmark also requires navigation. A contents page that lists sections, figures and tables. A method page. A document-control block. A running footer with the scope note. Page numbers. Headings that do not get orphaned at the bottom of a page. Tables that do not split across pages unless they have to. Margins that survive printing.

This is not typography for its own sake. If a scientist prints the dossier and cannot read it, the document has failed. If a table breaks across a page and loses its header, the reader has to guess what the columns mean. If a heading sits alone at the bottom of a page while its content starts on the next page, the document looks careless, and the reader starts to trust it less.

So the layout work is part of the evidence work. Presentation is not separate from rigour.

Now, the five reference dossiers.

The first is Dengue. Version 4.4. Sixty pages. Thirty-one sections. Thirty-three sources. Twenty-six claims. Seven tables. Six figures. It covers the four serotypes, the antibody-dependent enhancement problem, the vaccine landscape, the antiviral pipeline, the antiparasitic evidence, the vector-control evidence, the trial history, the gaps, and the decision.

The second is Friedreich ataxia. Version 4.0. Fifty-one pages. Thirty sections. Thirty sources. Twenty-five claims. Eight tables. Six figures. It covers the GAA repeat, frataxin, iron-sulfur cluster biology, the experimental structures, the cardiac and neurological progression, the trial landscape, the gene-therapy and protein-replacement routes, the gaps, and the decision.

The third is NSCLC with an EGFR-mutant focus. Version 4.0. Forty-nine pages. Thirty sections. Thirty sources. Twenty-five claims. Ten tables. Six figures. It covers the EGFR variants, the resistance mechanisms, the first-line and later-line evidence, the CNS question, the biomarker testing gap, the trial landscape, the combination strategies, the safety trade-offs, the gaps, and the decision.

The fourth is Gaucher disease. Version 2.0. Fifty-eight pages. Thirty sections. Thirty sources. Twenty-eight claims. Six tables. Six figures. It covers GBA1, glucocerebrosidase, the lysosomal biology, the neuronopathic question, the enzyme-replacement and substrate-reduction routes, the bone disease, the gene-therapy landscape, the monitoring questions, the gaps, and the decision.

The fifth is Fabry disease. Version 2.0. Fifty-three pages. Thirty-one sections. Thirty sources. Twenty-five claims. Six tables. Six figures. It covers GLA, alpha-galactosidase A, the lysosomal storage, the cardiac and renal progression, the enzyme-replacement and chaperone routes, the gene-therapy landscape, the trial evidence, the gaps, and the decision.

Five different diseases. Three rare diseases, one cancer, one infectious disease. The point is not that the template is rigid. The point is that the same standard can hold across all of them.

Now, I want to explain why thirty sections is the floor, not the target.

A short disease with a thin literature does not need padding. It needs honesty. If the evidence does not support thirty sections, the dossier should say so, and the sections that remain should be shorter, not invented. The benchmark is a quality floor for a full dossier, not a requirement to manufacture content.

That is an important distinction. We are not trying to make every disease look the same. We are trying to make every full dossier complete enough to be useful.

The sections also have a hierarchy. Some are load-bearing. Identity, mechanism, evidence, trials, decision. Some are supporting. Diagnostic pathway, natural history, patient burden, competitive landscape. Some are critical appraisal. Red team, gaps, falsifiers. The layout should make that hierarchy visible.

That is why the decision page is not buried at the end of a hundred pages. It is near the front, after the contents and method, and then repeated in full at the end. A reader should be able to find the recommendation without reading the whole document.

And the decision page has to be honest about its own status. “Conditional” is a valid recommendation. “Research needed” is a valid recommendation. “Do not pursue this route” is a valid recommendation. A dossier that always recommends a drug is not a dossier; it is a sales document.

Now, the claims.

Every claim gets an ID, a statement, a status, a confidence, a source or sources, and a date. The status vocabulary is not vague. Current. Stale. Conflict. Unverified. Blocked. Retracted. Those words mean something, and they are visible to the reader.

That matters because research changes. A trial reads out. A paper is retracted. A guideline is updated. A structure is superseded. A dossier that does not show its claim status is pretending that knowledge is static.

The daily research loop is designed around that. New papers and trials are scanned, and new findings enter a review queue. They do not silently rewrite a claim. A claim changes only after a source check and a version bump. That is slower, but it is the only way to keep the document trustworthy.

The source register is the backbone. Every claim points to a source ID. Every source has a URL and a retrieval date. Every figure and table points to the sources that support it. If a source cannot be resolved, the claim is marked, not hidden.

That is the audit trail.

There is one more layout rule that matters: the dossier has to work as a handoff document. A supervisor, a collaborator, a clinician or a reviewer should be able to open it without a guided tour. The cover tells them what it is. The contents tell them where to go. The method tells them how to read it. The claim ledger tells them what is contested. The decision page tells them what the document recommends and what would change it. If any one of those is missing, the document depends on the author being in the room, and that is not a durable research product.

That is why the new layout is versioned and dated. A dossier is not a one-off PDF. It is a record of what was known on a particular day, with a route to the next version.

Now, the red team.

Eight red-team items is the minimum. Each one is a genuine objection, not a rhetorical concession. “The evidence is limited” is not a red-team item. “The Phase 2 trial used a surrogate endpoint, was open-label, and enrolled twelve patients, so the effect size is unstable” is a red-team item.

The red team is where the document argues with itself. It is also where a reviewer should start, because it shows what the authors already know is weak.

That is unusual. Most documents hide their weak points in a limitations paragraph. We put them in the structure.

Now, the figures.

We require at least six factual figures. Not screenshots of a dashboard. Not decorative icons. Diagrams that carry information: a disease mechanism, a trial landscape, a structure coverage map, a diagnostic pathway, a therapeutic route comparison, an evidence map.

Each figure has a caption, a source line, and a reason to exist. If a figure does not help the reader understand something that the text cannot convey as efficiently, it should not be in the document.

The same rule applies to tables. A table is not a way to avoid writing. It is a way to make a comparison explicit. If the table has no numbers, no categories and no comparison, it is probably a list, and a list belongs in the text.

Now, the layout mechanics.

The body is set at a readable size, with generous margins, because this document is meant to be printed. The headings are navy, the body is black, the tables are compact but not microscopic. The header rows repeat when a table breaks across pages. Headings stay with their content. Orphan and widow control is on.

The cover is the only page with a full background. The interior pages are white, because ink is expensive and because a white page is easier to annotate. The cover carries the identity; the interior carries the evidence.

The contents page lists the sections and the exhibits. It does not invent page numbers if the renderer cannot calculate them. Showing no page number is better than showing a wrong one. That is a small example of a larger rule: do not fake precision.

The running footer carries the document title, the version, the page number, and the scope note. The scope note is not fine print to be hidden. It is part of the document’s identity.

Now, what does a reviewer actually receive?

They receive the full dossier, the decision brief, the claim ledger, and a short reviewer brief with ten questions. They do not receive the internal engine, the private evidence room, patient data, or any implementation detail. They are not asked for an endorsement, funding, or a favour. They are asked for criticism.

The ten questions are designed to be uncomfortable.

Is the causal claim precise enough?

Which source would you replace with a better primary study?

Is the leading hypothesis still the most testable one?

Which functional readout is accepted in your lab?

What would make you reject the first hypothesis?

Is the structural caveat sufficient?

Which trial or registry entry is missing?

What is the strongest objection to the recommendation?

What is missing before you would use the dossier?

Would this save you time, or is it another summary?

That last question is the one that matters. If the answer is “another summary,” the document has failed, no matter how many sections it has.

Now, I want to be clear about what the thirty-section layout is not.

It is not a template that fills itself. It is not a substitute for reading the primary literature. It is not a claim that every section is equally strong. It is not a guarantee that the recommendation is right.

It is a structure that makes the evidence visible, the uncertainty explicit, and the argument testable.

That is all it is. That is enough.

The engine stays private. The evidence and the dossier are public. That is the balance.

We are not selling the machine. We are selling what the machine produces: a cited, structured, reviewable research product. The dossier is the unit of value.

And the thirty sections are how we make that unit complete.

Let me close with the standard.

A good dossier should let a scientist do four things.

Find the evidence without hunting through ten tabs.

See where the evidence is weak without reading between the lines.

Disagree with a specific claim without rewriting the whole document.

Design the next experiment without starting from a blank page.

If the layout does that, it has done its job.

If it does not, no amount of cover design will save it.

That is the thirty-section dossier.

Research use only. Not medical advice. Every claim sourced, every section falsifiable, every version dated.

We are Sabre-Tek. We are the Light.

Thanks for listening.
