Let me begin with the hardest problem of all, because it is the one that defines the whole field.

The first hard problem is ab initio folding. The folding from scratch.

Remember chapter two, where we learned the distinction between supervised and ab initio folding. Supervised folding refines a known shape — and it works beautifully, scoring above point nine consistently. But ab initio folding is the true test — folding a protein from nothing but its sequence, with no template, no prior knowledge, just the physics.

And here is the honest truth. Our ab initio folding is not yet solved. It is our frontier. It is where we are weakest, and it is where the giants are strongest.

Let me explain why, because understanding the why is the key to understanding where we are.

The protein folding problem is hard because of something we met in chapter one — Levinthal’s paradox. A protein chain has an astronomical number of possible shapes. Far more than any computer could search one by one. So a folding system cannot just try everything. It has to be guided — by something that tells it where to look.

The giants — the most famous systems in the world — are guided by data. Enormous amounts of data. They have been trained on hundreds of thousands of known protein structures, and on the evolutionary relationships between millions of related sequences. That data gives them a shortcut. It tells them, from patterns they have learned, where the folded shape is likely to be.

Our system takes a different approach. We do physics. We simulate the forces, and we let the fold emerge. And that approach is beautiful, and it is honest, and it works magnificently for supervised folding, where we already have a shape to refine.

But for ab initio folding — from scratch — our physics needs help. Specifically, it needs the evolutionary data we discussed in chapter two. The contact map. The fingerprint that evolution leaves in related sequences, telling us which parts of the chain will end up near each other.

And here is the honest limit. To get that contact signal, we need hundreds of related sequences — hundreds of homologous proteins from different species, all aligned, all showing the same evolutionary pattern. When we have that, our physics can use it to guide the fold. When we do not, our physics alone is not yet enough, for large proteins, to find the correct shape from scratch.

We have measured this. We have run the experiments. And the honest result is this: our ab initio folding works well for small proteins, and it improves dramatically when we have deep evolutionary data. But for large proteins, folded from nothing, without that data — we are not yet there.

I want to be very clear about what this means, because it is the most important honest statement in this entire course. We do not claim to beat the giants. We claim to approach their accuracy on the proteins we can fold — the supervised, refined folds — at a fraction of the cost, with physics we can explain. And we claim to be honest, in writing, about the frontier we have not yet crossed.

The second hard problem is one we met in chapter eight, and it deserves its own place here, because it is the most personally instructive failure we have ever had. It is the beta-globin problem.

Beta-globin is one of the chains of haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in your blood. It is a small, well-studied, medically important protein. And it resists us.

Here is the story, because it teaches something important about honesty.

For a long time, we thought the problem was our computer. We thought we simply did not have enough power. That is a natural assumption — folding is computationally demanding, and it is easy to blame the hardware.

But we were wrong. And we proved it.

We ran beta-globin and its sister chain, alpha-globin, on the same machine, with the same settings, in the same amount of time. Same hardware. Same software. Same everything. And here is what happened. Alpha-globin folded beautifully — a score near point nine six. Beta-globin did not. It stalled, stuck in the wrong shape.

Same machine. Same settings. Same everything. One chain folds, the other does not.

That is the proof. The problem was not the computer. It was the biology. One chain is simply harder to fold than the other.

And this is the lesson, the one I want you to hold on to from this entire chapter. When something does not work, it is easy to blame the machine. It is easy to say “we need more power.” But sometimes — often — the truth is more subtle. The problem is not the hardware. It is the problem itself. And the only way to find that out is to run the experiment, honestly, and look at what actually happened.

We could have hidden the beta-globin failure. We could have quietly moved on to easier proteins and never mentioned it. Most teams do exactly that. Instead, we studied it. We wrote it down. We shared it. Because understanding why something fails is how you eventually make it succeed.

And we are making progress on it. We found a way — a technique called multi-start, where we run the fold many times from different starting points — that lifts beta-globin from a stalled score to a much better one. Not perfect. Not yet at the alpha-globin level. But better. And we are honest that the gap remains.

The third hard problem is the one that connects to everything — the gap between design and cure.

This is the widest, most important gap of all, and I want to be completely plain about it. Everything we build — the folds, the gates, the modalities, the databases, the dossiers — is computational. It is prediction. It is design. It is not yet medicine.

A real drug is not a molecule on a screen. A real drug is a molecule that has been tested in a laboratory, in cells, in animals, in human volunteers, in large clinical trials, over years, at enormous cost, with every step scrutinised and validated. That is the path from a design to a cure, and it is long and hard.

We have not walked that path. Our AUTOTAC designs — the smart missiles from chapter five — are beautiful, and clever, and grounded in real, published science. But they have not been tested in a cell. They have not been given to a patient. They are predictions, not treatments.

And here is the honest truth about what that means. Our system can help. It can fold proteins. It can identify mechanisms. It can design molecules. It can check those designs against databases, and flag their risks, and point to the next steps. That is real, and it is valuable.

But it cannot, today, cure anyone. And we say so. On every report. In plain language. Because the greatest danger in this field is not a wrong number. It is overclaiming. It is the temptation to say “we have a cure” when all we have is a prediction.
