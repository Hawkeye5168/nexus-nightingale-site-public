Welcome to chapter three. In chapter two we asked how to obtain a shape. Now we ask the more important medical question: what is the protein doing wrong?

The gate is a mechanism check. It is not a diagnosis, and it is not a treatment recommendation. It is a structured question that decides which kinds of intervention are even worth considering.

The first possibility is loss of function. The protein is missing, reduced, unstable or unable to do its job. Friedreich ataxia is the example we keep returning to: frataxin is depleted, so the route is restoration — a frataxin upregulator, a gene therapy, a protein-replacement strategy — not degradation. Degrading a protein that is already missing would make the disease worse.

The second is gain of function. The protein is overactive, constitutively switched on, or carrying a mutation that removes its normal brake. EGFR-mutant lung cancer is the canonical example. The target is driving the cancer, so inhibition or degradation can be rational — but only if the resistance context and the therapeutic window support it.

The third is toxic aggregation. The problem is not simply too much activity; it is a misfolded or aggregated species causing damage. Huntington disease is the familiar example. The therapeutic question is whether reducing the toxic species, improving clearance, or protecting the affected cells is the most testable route.

The fourth is haploinsufficiency. One working copy is not enough. The route is usually to increase the output of the remaining allele, not to inhibit it.

There are other categories — trafficking defects, dominant-negative effects, RNA toxicity, metabolic accumulation and diseases where the primary problem is not a single protein at all. The gate should not force every disease into a protein-shaped hole. When the mechanism is unknown, the honest output is research needed.

This is where many computational systems fail. They see a protein and assume the answer is a small molecule that inhibits it. That assumption is wrong for a large part of rare disease. A loss-of-function enzyme does not need an inhibitor. A toxic aggregate may not respond to a clean active-site blocker. A splicing defect may need an RNA-directed approach. A missing protein may need replacement or gene therapy.

So the gate produces a direction, not a molecule. It says: protect, restore, reduce, replace, edit, silence, support, or stop and investigate. The modality chapter turns that direction into a toolbox.

And the gate has a compassion rule. If the mechanism is loss of function, do not issue a degrader. If the evidence is not there, do not invent a mechanism. If the route would make the patient worse, refuse it explicitly. A refusal is a result. It is often a more useful result than a confident wrong answer.
