Welcome to chapter four. The gate has told us what kind of problem we have. Now we need a toolbox broad enough to answer it.

It is tempting to think of drug discovery as “find a small molecule that binds the protein.” That is one route, and a very successful one. It is not the only route, and for rare disease it is often the wrong first question.

The current NEXUS modality map has thirteen routes. They are not all equally mature, and they are not all appropriate for every target. The point is to make the choice explicit.

  • Small-molecule inhibitors or activators — oral, well-understood Chemistry, Manufacturing and Controls, but often limited by resistance or by the need for a deep pocket.
  • Antibodies and biologics — high selectivity and a long half-life, but usually extracellular or accessible-surface targets, with immunogenicity and manufacturing considerations.
  • Antibody–drug conjugates — a targeting antibody plus a cytotoxic payload; powerful in oncology, but the payload and linker bring their own toxicity.
  • Targeted degraders — molecules that recruit a destruction pathway to remove a protein rather than merely block it. Research-grade until the selectivity, exposure and safety package are shown.
  • Molecular glues — small molecules that create or stabilise a new protein–protein interaction; elegant, but highly context-dependent.
  • Gene therapy — deliver a working copy of a gene; the delivery vehicle, tissue specificity and durability are the hard parts.
  • Gene editing — correct or disable a genomic sequence; precise in principle, with delivery, off-target and long-term safety questions.
  • RNA-directed therapies — antisense oligonucleotides or RNA interference to alter splicing or reduce a transcript; delivery and durability are central.
  • Protein replacement — supply the missing protein directly; established for some lysosomal diseases, but tissue access and immunogenicity matter.
  • Cell therapy — engineer or supply cells that perform a missing function; complex, expensive and still early for most diseases.
  • Chaperones — small molecules that help a misfolded protein fold or traffic correctly; useful for selected trafficking defects.
  • Substrate reduction — reduce the material that accumulates in a storage disorder; a metabolic route rather than a direct protein hit.
  • Symptomatic and supportive care — treat the consequence when disease modification is not yet available; a legitimate route, not a failure of imagination.

The list is deliberately modality-agnostic. A rare disease may need an enzyme replacement; a cancer may need a TKI; a splicing disorder may need an antisense oligonucleotide; a channelopathy may need a small-molecule opener or closer. The mechanism picks the shortlist, not the fashion of the year.

Each route has a different evidence package. A small molecule needs exposure, selectivity and a safety window. A gene therapy needs a delivery vector, a dose–response and long-term expression data. An antibody–drug conjugate needs target selection, linker stability and payload toxicity. A degrader needs productive target engagement, selectivity and a demonstration that removal of the protein actually helps.

That is why the pipeline does not stop at “modality selected.” It records why the route was chosen, what would make it fail, and what experiment would test it. A route is a hypothesis about the right kind of intervention, not a promise that the intervention works.
