Welcome to chapter six. The previous chapters described the biology, the gate and the toolbox. This chapter is about why you should believe any of it.

The answer is not “because the model is confident.” The answer is evidence: named sources, dates, evidence tiers and visible gaps. The current NEXUS evidence layer draws on twenty-four biomedical sources. They are not all the same kind of source, and they are not all equally strong.

The sources fall into families:

  • Identity and sequence — UniProt, NCBI Gene and related reference records.
  • Structure — the PDB and experimental structural metadata.
  • Disease and phenotype — Orphanet, OMIM, HPO and curated disease records.
  • Variants and clinical interpretation — ClinVar and related resources.
  • Chemistry — PubChem, ChEMBL and compound records.
  • Pathways and interactions — Reactome and protein-interaction resources.
  • Trials and regulation — ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA labels and regulatory records.
  • Literature — PubMed and primary papers.
  • Safety and pharmacology — adverse-event, pharmacokinetic and interaction resources.
  • Internal curated sources — the project’s own evidence, design and audit records.

Every factual claim in a dossier should resolve to one of those sources, or it should be marked as unsupported. A source is not a decoration at the bottom of a page. It is the thing that lets a reader disagree with you precisely.

The evidence tier matters. A primary experimental paper is not the same as a registry entry. A registry entry is not the same as a preprint. A model output is not the same as a biological measurement. The dossier uses tiers so the reader can see the difference without having to reconstruct your judgement.

The current catalogue contains 11,645 clinical entities. That is a catalogue, not 11,645 validated diseases. 4,245 gene-associated therapeutic-stage runs have been performed. That is a measure of coverage, not a measure of efficacy. Fifteen public disease pages exist; five benchmark dossiers go deeper. The distinction between a catalogue record, a public summary and a benchmark dossier is deliberate.

Each dossier also carries a claim ledger. Every claim has an ID, a statement, a status, a confidence, a source list and a last-checked date. The status vocabulary is deliberately blunt: CURRENT, STALE, CONFLICT, UNVERIFIED, BLOCKED, RETRACTED. A claim that cannot be checked does not become “probably true.” It becomes visible as unresolved.

The daily research loop scans the catalogue and the dossier watch terms. New papers and trials enter a review queue. They do not silently rewrite a claim. A claim changes only after a source check and a version bump. That is slower than automatic updating, and it is the only way to keep the document trustworthy.

The rule is simple: no source, no claim; no date, no confidence. The next chapter shows how those rules become a dossier.
