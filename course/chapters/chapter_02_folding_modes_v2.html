Welcome to chapter two. In chapter one we established that sequence is information and shape is function. Now we have to be precise about how NEXUS obtains a shape, because this is where a lot of computational biology quietly overclaims.

There are three useful modes, and they are not interchangeable.

Supervised folding starts with a sequence and a known experimental structure. The job is to reconstruct that structure — or something close to it — under a named set of conditions. This is the production path when a reference exists. It is a reconstruction test, not a blind prediction. We report the reference PDB entry, the construct, the TM-score, the RMSD and the caveat attached to that comparison.

Ab initio folding starts with the sequence and no template. This is the research frontier. It is not the production path, and it is not presented as a validated blind-prediction engine. When the evidence is thin, the honest output is a research model with a stated limit, not a confident structure.

AlphaFold refinement starts from an external model and asks whether the geometry can be made more physically sensible. It is useful, but the starting model is external. We say so. We do not present a refined external model as if it were built from nothing.

The crucial distinction is between a reconstruction and a prediction. A high score against a known structure tells you the engine can recover that structure under those conditions. It does not tell you the engine will recover an unknown protein equally well. Those are different claims, and they require different evidence.

There is also a data problem underneath all three modes. Supervised folding is limited by the quality of the reference and by how well the construct matches the biological question. Ab initio folding is limited by evolutionary information: the multiple sequence alignment. If the alignment is shallow, the contact map is weak, and the model has less to work with. A deep alignment is not a luxury; it is part of the evidence.

That is why the pipeline reports MSA depth rather than hiding it. A structure built on a shallow alignment should be read differently from one built on a deep, diverse family. The number matters, but so does the coverage and the identity range. A count alone is not enough.

The output also has to be described correctly. A kinase-domain construct is not the full receptor in a cell. A fragment is not the whole protein. A predicted model is not an experimental measurement. A model can be useful for generating a hypothesis and still be wrong about the detail that decides whether a drug binds.

So the rule is simple: name the mode, name the reference, name the limit. If we cannot say which of those three things we are doing, we are not ready to show a structure.

In the next chapter we move from shape to mechanism. A structure tells us where a molecule might bind. It does not tell us whether we should destroy the protein, protect it, replace it, or leave it alone. That question is the gate.
