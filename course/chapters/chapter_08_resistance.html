Welcome to chapter eight. This chapter is about the hardest problem in cancer medicine, and arguably the hardest problem in all of medicine. It is about resistance — the way diseases fight back against our drugs.

Everything we have built so far — the fold, the gate, the modalities, the databases, the dossier — all of it is an attempt to win a war. But this chapter is about the enemy’s side of that war. Because the enemy does not sit still. The enemy evolves. And understanding how it evolves is essential to understanding why our approach — targeted protein degradation — matters so much.

Let me start with the fundamental idea, and it is a beautiful and terrifying one. Evolution.

You probably think of evolution as something that happens over millions of years, to animals and plants. But evolution also happens inside a single patient, over the course of months, inside a tumour. Because cancer cells reproduce rapidly, and each time they divide, they make small copying errors — mutations. Most of those mutations are harmless, or harmful to the cell. But occasionally, by pure chance, one mutation gives a cancer cell an advantage. And when we apply a drug, the advantage we care about is this: the mutation lets the cell survive the drug.

Here is how it works, step by step, and I want you to really see the logic, because it is the logic of the whole chapter.

Imagine a tumour made of billions of cancer cells. Most of those cells carry the same mutation that is driving the cancer — say, the EGFR mutation we have discussed, the switch stuck on. Now we give the patient a drug — osimertinib, say — that blocks that mutated protein. The drug works. The cancer cells, which depend on that stuck-on switch, start to die. The tumour shrinks. The patient responds. It looks like victory.

But wait. Among those billions of cells, there is variety. Because cancer cells mutate constantly, a few of them — a tiny fraction — have accumulated additional mutations. And one of those rare mutations, in one rare cell, changes the shape of the EGFR protein just enough that osimertinib can no longer bind to it. The key no longer fits the lock.

When the drug kills all the cells that are vulnerable to it, that one resistant cell is left. And now it has no competition. It divides, and divides, and the tumour that grows back is made entirely of cells that are resistant to the drug.

This is resistance. The drug worked — until it did not. The cancer evolved, and escaped.

And here is the cruel beauty of it. The drug did not fail. The drug did exactly what it was designed to do. It is just that the enemy, under the pressure of the drug, found a way around it. This is the arms race of medicine — and we are in it.

Now let me make it concrete, because abstract logic only becomes real when you see the actual mutations. Let me look at the most famous resistance story in cancer medicine: the EGFR mutations.

EGFR, as we know, is a receptor that drives cancer when it is mutated. The first drugs against it were called first-generation inhibitors — gefitinib and erlotinib. They blocked the mutated EGFR, and they worked — for a while.

Then the cancer evolved. The most common resistance mutation was a single change, called T790M. The name is a code: threonine at position 790, changed to methionine. One amino acid swapped for another, out of over a thousand. And that one swap changed the shape of the protein just enough that the first-generation drugs could no longer bind.

Why? Let me understand the mechanism, because it is genuinely elegant. The first-generation drugs work by fitting into the protein’s pocket and blocking its activity. But T790M changed the pocket. Specifically, the mutation changed a gatekeeper residue — a single amino acid that sits like a guard at the entrance to the pocket. The new, larger methionine blocked the drug’s entry. The key no longer fit.

So scientists built a second generation of drugs — afatinib and dacomitinib — designed to bind more tightly. They worked better, but the cancer found a way around them too.

Then came the breakthrough: a third-generation drug called osimertinib. Osimertinib was specifically designed to bind to the T790M mutant. It is a covalent drug — it forms a permanent chemical bond with the protein, not just a temporary grip. This was a triumph. Osimertinib became the standard of care for EGFR-mutant lung cancer, and it transformed survival.

But the arms race did not end. Because the cancer evolved again.

The resistance to osimertinib came in the form of another mutation, called C797S — cysteine at position 797, changed to serine. And the mechanism is heartbreakingly elegant. Osimertinib works by forming a covalent bond with a specific amino acid — a cysteine, at position 797. It latches onto that cysteine. But the C797S mutation removes that cysteine — replaces it with a serine — so osimertinib has nothing to latch onto. The covalent bond can no longer form. The drug loses its grip.

So here we are. The first-generation drugs were defeated by T790M. The third-generation drug was defeated by C797S. Each time, one mutation, one amino acid change, undid years of work.

And here is the truly daunting part, the part that makes this the hardest problem in medicine. The cancer can stack these mutations. A single tumour can carry T790M and C797S at the same time — and when it does, on the same copy of the gene, it is resistant to the first-generation drugs, and to the second-generation drugs, and to the third-generation drug. All of them. There is no available drug that can touch it.

This is not a hypothetical. It happens. It is called the cis T790M-C797S double mutant, and it represents an absolute wall in EGFR-targeted therapy. The patient, who once responded beautifully, is now out of options.

And there are more. The cancer can change the very shape of the gene — insertions, called exon 20 insertions, that wedge a piece of extra protein into the pocket and block every drug. The cancer can even change its entire identity — a lung cancer can transform into a small cell lung cancer, abandoning EGFR entirely, so that all EGFR drugs become useless.

This is the arms race. And now I want you to understand why this matters for our system, and why it is the reason we built what we built.

Here is the key insight, and it is the heart of this chapter. All of the drugs I have just described — gefitinib, erlotinib, afatinib, osimertinib — are blockers. They bind to EGFR and stop it from working. And because they only block, they are vulnerable to resistance. The cancer mutates the shape of the pocket, and the blocker can no longer bind. The key no longer fits the lock, and the lock has changed.

But our approach — targeted protein degradation, the AUTOTAC from chapter five — is fundamentally different. It does not just block the protein. It destroys the protein. It removes the lock entirely, rather than trying to jam it.

And here is why that matters for resistance. A blocker needs to bind tightly to one specific shape, to fit the pocket precisely. If the pocket changes shape, the blocker fails. But a degrader is more forgiving. It needs to grab the protein and mark it for destruction. Even if the protein mutates somewhat, the degrader can often still grab it, still tag it, still hand it to the cell’s recycling machinery. Destroying is more robust than blocking.

This is not to say that degraders are immune to resistance — nothing is. The cancer can still evolve, can still find a way. But the degrader approach is a fundamentally more robust strategy in the arms race. It is a different kind of weapon, one that the enemy’s usual dodges do not defeat as easily.

And this is the deeper point, the one I want you to hold on to. The arms race between drugs and cancer is not a fair fight. Cancer has billions of cells, each a dice roll, each a chance to find the mutation that escapes our drug. We, on the other hand, have to design each drug deliberately, over years, at enormous cost. Cancer explores. We plan. And in a race between exploration and planning, exploration often wins.

So the only way to stay ahead is to change the nature of the game. Not just to make better blockers, but to make a fundamentally different kind of weapon. A weapon that does not just jam the lock, but removes it. A weapon that the cancer’s usual dodges do not defeat.

That is what targeted protein degradation is. That is why AUTOTAC matters. It is our answer to the arms race — not a better key, but a way to remove the lock entirely.

Now let me also be honest, because honesty is our foundation. We do not yet know whether our degraders will ultimately win the arms race. The cancer may find a way around them too — perhaps by changing the protein so much that even our grabber cannot hold it, or by evolving a way to escape the recycling machinery. We do not claim victory. What we claim is a genuinely different strategy, one grounded in a real understanding of how resistance works, and one that addresses the fundamental weakness of blocking.

And that is enough to matter. Because in an arms race, the side that keeps fighting with the same weapon loses. The side that changes weapons has a chance.

Let me bring chapter eight together. Resistance is evolution, happening inside a single patient, over months. Cancer cells mutate, and under the pressure of a drug, the rare cell that can survive the drug takes over. We saw it happen, step by step, with EGFR — the first-generation drugs defeated by T790M, the third-generation drug defeated by C797S, and the terrifying double mutant that resists everything. All of those drugs were blockers, and blockers are vulnerable to the changing shape of the pocket. Our answer is degradation — removing the protein entirely, rather than jamming it — a fundamentally more robust strategy in the arms race.
