Welcome to chapter seven. We have come a long way. We learned how to fold a protein, how to decide whether to destroy it or protect it, how to choose a route, and how to check every claim against twenty-four evidence sources.

Now we arrive at the output. The thing we hand to a person. The document that is the whole point of everything we have built.

It is called the dossier. And in this chapter I am going to walk you through the current structure, section by section, so that when you hold one in your hands you know exactly what you are looking at and exactly where to find what you need.

The old edition of this chapter described a ten-part dossier. That was a useful start, but it is no longer the standard. A serious research question has more than ten jobs to do. The current standard is the thirty-section dossier, codified as Sabre-Tek Dossier Benchmark v1.

Thirty is a floor for a full dossier, not a target to pad toward. If the evidence cannot support thirty substantive sections, the right answer is a shorter, honest document — not invented content. The benchmark is a quality floor, not a licence to manufacture words.

The document starts with four pieces of furniture. A cover carrying the disease, the dossier ID, the version, the date and the scope note. A document-control block saying what version this is, what it supersedes and what standard it claims to meet. A contents page listing sections, figures and tables. And a method page explaining how the evidence was assembled, how sources were tiered, how claims were status-marked and what the document does not do.

Those four pages are not bureaucracy. They stop a reader mistaking a research product for a clinical opinion, and they stop two people arguing about two different versions without noticing.

Then come the sections. They are grouped into eight jobs.

Identity. What is the disease? What are its synonyms? What is the gene? What is the inheritance pattern? What is the affected protein? What is the clinical burden? What is the diagnostic pathway? If the identity is wrong, every later section is built on the wrong object.

Mechanism. What is the causal biology? Is this loss of function, gain of function, a toxic aggregate, a haploinsufficiency, a splicing defect, a metabolic block? What is the evidence for that mechanism? What is still unknown? This is where the dossier earns its keep, because the mechanism determines whether a therapeutic route is protective or dangerous.

Structure. What experimental structures exist? What do they cover? What is missing? What is the quality of the evidence? If a structure is a fragment, a construct or a model, the dossier says so. It does not blur a crystal structure, a cryo-EM map, a predicted model and a cartoon into one confident picture.

Evidence base. What does the primary literature say? What do the curated databases say? What do the trials say? What do the natural-history studies say? Where are the conflicts? Which claims are current, which are stale, which are unverified, which are blocked?

Therapeutic landscape. What routes are plausible? What has been tried? What failed? What is approved, and for whom? What is the evidence for the route, as distinct from the evidence for the target? This is where the thirteen-route toolbox becomes a shortlist, not a catalogue.

Trial and patient context. What trials are recruiting? What phase? What endpoints? What biomarkers? What prior lines? What populations are included and excluded? This is research context. It is not an eligibility decision, and the dossier says that plainly.

Critical appraisal. What are the strongest objections? What would a sceptical reviewer say? What data would overturn the recommendation? What is the next experiment? This is the red team, and it is not a limitations paragraph. It is a structure.

Decision. What is the recommendation? What are the alternatives? What are the conditions? What would change it? What should happen next, and who should do it? “Conditional” is a valid recommendation. “Research needed” is a valid recommendation. “Do not pursue this route” is a valid recommendation.

Thirty sections is not an arbitrary number. It is the number of distinct jobs that kept appearing when we pressure-tested the document. The exact section list is maintained in the benchmark specification, but the sequence is always identity first, decision last, with the evidence and the countercase in between.

Every section carries three depth elements: a key finding, at least one paragraph of analysis, and a falsifier — the evidence that would change the conclusion. A section without a falsifier is a summary, not an argument.

The benchmark also sets a floor for the exhibits. At least six factual figures. At least six quantitative tables. At least thirty unique sources. At least twenty-five claims. At least eight red-team items. Figures are diagrams that carry information, not decoration. Tables are comparisons with numbers, not lists of drug names.

Each figure and table carries its own sources. If a section has an exhibit that already carries the citations, the section-level source line is suppressed. One citation block per exhibit. No source-only pages. A page that repeats the source list from the page before is a defect, not a feature.

The claim ledger is the backbone. Every claim gets an ID, a statement, a status, a confidence, a source or sources, and a date. The status vocabulary is deliberately blunt: CURRENT, STALE, CONFLICT, UNVERIFIED, BLOCKED, RETRACTED. A claim that cannot be checked does not become “probably true.” It becomes visible as unresolved.

The layout is part of the evidence. The body is set at a readable size, with generous margins, because the document is meant to be printed. The headings are navy, the body is black, the tables are compact but not microscopic. Header rows repeat when a table breaks across pages. Headings stay with their content. Orphan and widow control are on.

The cover is the only page with a full background. The interior pages are white, because ink is expensive and because a white page is easier to annotate. The cover carries the identity; the interior carries the evidence.

The contents page lists the sections and the exhibits. It does not invent page numbers if the renderer cannot calculate them. Showing no page number is better than showing a wrong one. That is a small example of a larger rule: do not fake precision.

The running footer carries the document title, the version, the page number and the scope note. The scope note is not fine print to be hidden. It is part of the document’s identity.

Now, the five reference dossiers. Dengue 4.4 is sixty pages, thirty-one sections, thirty-three sources, twenty-six claims, seven tables and six figures. Friedreich ataxia 4.0 is fifty-one pages, thirty sections, thirty sources, twenty-five claims, eight tables and six figures. NSCLC 4.0 is forty-nine pages, thirty sections, thirty sources, twenty-five claims, ten tables and six figures. Gaucher 2.0 is fifty-eight pages, thirty sections, thirty sources, twenty-eight claims, six tables and six figures. Fabry 2.0 is fifty-three pages, thirty-one sections, thirty sources, twenty-five claims, six tables and six figures.

Five different diseases. Three rare diseases, one cancer, one infectious disease. The point is not that the template is rigid. The point is that the same standard can hold across all of them.

What does a reviewer actually receive? The full dossier, the decision brief, the claim ledger and a short reviewer brief with ten questions. They do not receive the internal engine, the private evidence room, patient data or any implementation detail. They are not asked for an endorsement, funding or a favour. They are asked for criticism.

The ten questions are designed to be uncomfortable. Is the decision question the right question? Which claim is the weakest? Is any source being asked to support more than it says? Is the recommendation properly conditional? Is the primary endpoint clinically meaningful? What is missing from the target product profile? Does the dossier overstate coverage, structural evidence or biomarker readiness? What would make it unusable for a serious partner? Is the red team strong enough? What one experiment would most change the decision?

That last question is the one that matters. If the answer is “another summary,” the document has failed, no matter how many sections it has.

The dossier is not a sales document. It is an honesty document. It does not try to make the drug look good. It tries to tell the truth about the drug — what it is, what it does, what could go wrong, what we know and what we do not know.

That is the thirty-section dossier. Not a longer report. A structured argument with the evidence attached and the uncertainty visible.
